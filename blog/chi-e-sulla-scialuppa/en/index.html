Climate & denial · 11 September 2026 · 9 min read

Who's on the lifeboat

We're not psychologists. We have no credentials to tell anyone how they're doing. We open our phones in the morning, read that it was the hottest month ever recorded, and go to work anyway.

On 22 August 2026 Cristiano Godano, singer of the Italian band Marlene Kuntz, published a piece that stayed with us.[1] Here we try to pick up those arguments from below. From the crowd, which is where we live.

We felt it before we read it

The strangest part isn't the data. It's that a huge number of people had worked it out before seeing any of it. You feel it in an October that still smells like August, in sea water that never cools down, in seasons that seem to have gone from four to two. It's a vague, almost superstitious perception, as if a crooked zodiac were assembling over our heads for us to read our future in.

Normally science's job is to take feelings like that apart and send us home calmer. This time it's confirming them one by one, with a coldness we keep out of our line of sight. Europe is warming at roughly twice the global average: it's the fastest-warming continent, and we live inside it. The summer that made you swear is, in all likelihood, the coolest one you'll get from here on.

The boiled frog doesn't even work as a metaphor

Everyone knows the story: a frog in slowly heating water doesn't jump out and ends up cooked. It's worth knowing it's false. Frogs jump out, the nineteenth-century experiments behind the anecdote were badly run, and biologists have been saying so for decades. That we keep telling it says something about us rather than about amphibians: we like an image where the fault lies with a natural perception glitch and nobody decided anything.

Because on the human side the mechanism really does exist, it's just less comfortable. Our brain is tuned for sudden threats, for the rustle in the bushes. A curve rising by fractions of a degree per year triggers no biological alarm at all. What's needed is a cultural alarm, meaning method and critical thinking. Which nobody, at any level of schooling, ever taught us systematically.

Repression would have been the good news

This is the passage that made us sit down. Talking about this stuff we always reach for the word repression: September brings cooler air and we repress everything until the next nightmare. But repression, in the technical sense, is something else, and it's almost reassuring. Repressing means you know, the thing hurts, and your mind puts it in the basement. It stays down there, it pushes, it comes back in dreams, in anxiety, in symptoms. Whoever represses, deep down, knows.

What we see around us looks more like a different defence, the one Freud called Verleugnung, usually translated as disavowal or denial. Denial doesn't hide the information, it declares it void. The data came in, the perception happened, and the mind decides it doesn't count. It's a more archaic mechanism, and when it organises itself, when it becomes impermeable to any refutation and builds a coherent counter-reality around itself with its own heroes and enemies, clinicians start using a very heavy word: delusion.

Climate denialism is rarely ignorance. The information is free, everywhere, checkable by anyone with a connection. And the people who practise it are often educated, sharp, able to argue for hours. Bought-off elites, plots to shut down the factories, the Chinese waiting to pick up the pieces: if a structure like that walked into a doctor's office carried by a single person, it would make anyone's antennae go up.

Doubt is an industrial product, with its own R&D department

One detail makes the whole thing less psychiatric and more criminal: this denial didn't sprout on its own. Oil companies had accurate internal climate models as early as the late 1970s. Not hunches, not vague scenarios: projections that, re-examined in 2023 in a study published in Science by Supran, Rahmstorf and Oreskes, turned out to be as accurate as those of academic scientists at the time. They knew, and to a good approximation they knew when.

Then they spent decades of money manufacturing doubt, using the same playbook the tobacco industry had used before them. Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway wrote Merchants of Doubt about it. Sociologist Stanley Cohen devoted his most important book, States of Denial, precisely to how entire societies manage to know and not know at the same time. We're not talking about a spontaneous collective hallucination. We're talking about a product, with a budget, suppliers and a return on investment.

Why well-made videos achieve so little

This is the part that concerns us directly, because we're among those who share links hoping it helps. Anyone who works on behaviour change knows it: information alone almost never produces change. No smoker ever quit because someone read the warning label out loud to them. Knowing isn't enough, and never was.

Let alone when the defence in front of you has, as its specific function, the annulment of information. You can make a thousand impeccable pieces, with sources at the bottom and the right charts, and you're still carrying water with a teaspoon into a fire. On the other side there's an apparatus producing denial at planetary scale, with algorithms that reward whoever reassures and bury whoever unsettles. There's a line usually attributed to Mark Twain, almost certainly not his, that remains unbeaten: it's far easier to fool people than to convince them they've been fooled.

The lifeboats are already in the water, and they paid for them

Fine, the useful idiots. But the billionaires? They have the best reports on the planet sitting on their desks, handsomely paid for. What's going on in their heads?

You don't need any conspiracy to form an idea, and you don't need a diagnosis from afar either, which American psychiatrists have considered improper since 1973 under what they call the Goldwater rule. Just listen to what they say in public, proudly, on stage. Jeff Bezos has been repeating his vision for years: move heavy industry off the planet, build orbital habitats for millions of people, and leave Earth to residential and light use, a sort of protected zone. Elon Musk says the goal of his life is to make humanity a multiplanetary species, with a self-sustaining city on Mars as soon as possible.

Put that way they sound like cover-story visions. Read them again watching behaviour instead of statements and they sound different. If you're genuinely convinced your species' future is elsewhere, what value does the place you're standing in still have? The value of an asset to squeeze in order to fund the departure. Someone who has decided to move out doesn't renovate the house, they empty it.

Christopher Lasch, in the mid-nineties and before the internet, called this dynamic the revolt of the elites: ruling classes detaching themselves from the common fate and no longer feeling they're in the same boat. He was thinking of gated communities and tax havens. He hadn't seen the rockets yet.

Two hundred heads in liquid nitrogen

There's a piece that makes the picture stranger still. The same men divesting from the planet are obsessed with not dying.

Ernest Becker, in a book that won him the Pulitzer, argued that all of human civilisation is a project of symbolic immortality, an elaborate way of not looking our own end in the face. Out of that came an experimental line of research, Terror Management Theory, which showed something chilling: remind people they're going to die and they become more materialistic and more attached to consumption. Hold that thought, then look at the top of the pyramid.

In Arizona there's a facility where more than two hundred bodies, and in plenty of cases only heads, float in liquid nitrogen at a hundred and ninety-six degrees below zero. They're waiting for a revival technology for which not a shred of evidence exists. Bryan Johnson, the entrepreneur with the Don't Die T-shirt, had his teenage son's plasma transfused into himself and later admitted it did nothing. Billions rain down on cellular reprogramming labs, and to be clear: ageing research is serious, interesting science. That isn't the problem. The problem is what's built on top of it, and who funds it hoping to buy off the last inequality left.

They want to live forever, and to manage it they're making the place everyone else will live in uninhabitable.

1816: the wine was all on the raft

On 2 July 1816 the French frigate Méduse ran aground on sandbanks off the coast of Mauritania. There weren't enough lifeboats. Officers, notables and the governor took their places in the boats. For roughly a hundred and forty-seven people, soldiers and lower-ranking passengers, a makeshift raft was assembled which the boats were supposed to tow ashore.

A few miles out, someone on the boats cut the ropes.

Thirteen days later, when the raft was found, fifteen people were alive on board. In the meantime there had been madness, thirst, mutiny, violence, cannibalism. One detail usually goes unnoticed: the raft carried very little food and a lot of wine, and the first night ended in a drunken brawl. Géricault's painting in the Louvre comes from this, and it scandalised the whole of France.

Today we have cheap dopamine, infinite scrolling, summers of purpose-built entertainment, ice-cold drinks so we don't think about the heat. The system producing the anguish is the same one selling us the sedation. The wine, as in 1816, is all on the raft.

We have no consoling ending, but we do have one correction

Godano closes hoping the anguish will wake us up. The trouble is that anguish on its own doesn't mobilise: it produces learned helplessness, the condition Seligman and Maier described in their 1967 experiments, in which an organism, after enough failed attempts, simply stops trying.

Here, though, there's a correction that matters a great deal to us, as ordinary people staying on the raft. In the experiments helplessness doesn't come from the danger: it comes from the absence of a perceived way out. Perceived control is what makes the difference, and perception can be built. Someone with a lever, however small, however ridiculous against the scale of the problem, doesn't shut down the same way.

We're not going to manufacture hope by the kilo while the behaviour of those in charge tells another story. That would just be a gentler form of denial. But we do know that keeping a clear head is already a position, that refusing to spend your attention where someone is selling it is already a lever, and that whatever each of us actually controls, however little that is, is the one place where learned helplessness doesn't take hold.

Next time you watch a rocket lift off, and feel that shiver of wonder we feel too, try asking yourself the question that's been going round our heads for days. Not who built it. Who's on board.
