You can't sell a hug

We all say it: we have everything. And it's true. There's never been this much technology, this much stuff to read, this many ways to reach anyone at any moment. Then you look up and there's an emptiness running through your day, and you pretend not to notice.

When I stop and think about what's actually missing, one thing comes to mind: real empathy. The genuine kind. Because of the fake kind we have plenty. The little hearts, the "I'm here for you", the lines tossed off in three seconds between one notification and the next, now written by AI too. We've got mountains of that, and it's useless.

That's the point. We live inside a stream of relationships that are fast, shallow, on and off. And we feel it. We've got very good at telling when someone actually cares about us and when they're just waiting for their turn to talk. Connected, sure. Close, no.

Has it ever happened to you, being surrounded by people and still feeling alone? Not the loneliness of having nobody around, that one you spot straight away. I mean the other kind: you're in a full room, maybe with people who are genuinely fond of you, and you feel like the wrong piece wedged in there. Not understood, out of place, not good enough.

It's happened to me plenty of times. Evenings everyone else loved that after a couple of hours turned into a weight for me. Not because anyone was unpleasant, quite the opposite. It's that at some point you see them, the masks we all wear. Pirandello wrote about it a century ago and he was right. And once you've seen them you can't unsee them: you're talking to the part the other person decided to show, and meanwhile you're doing exactly the same thing.

A brain built for another world

It's something I think about a lot: certain ways of living that we now take for granted feel unnatural to me. We didn't choose them, they were forced on us by the environment we were born and raised in. Our brain took shape in small groups, at slow speeds, face to face. Today that same brain lives in a world running at an absurd pace, a place that's alien to it.

That distance, between how we're built and how we live, is where I think a lot of the suffering I see around me starts. Because suffering is almost never a defect sitting inside you. It sits in the space between you and what's around you. So let's take a good look at it. The way I see it, the problem is three things.

First: we're always competing. A constant race, never declared, fed by the comparing that social media multiplied to infinity. Other people stop being fellow travellers and become a ruler to measure yourself against, someone to envy. And you can't feel compassion for someone your brain is using as a yardstick, in a race nobody chose but everybody runs.

Second: we're always distracted. Empathy needs attention, and attention is exactly what gets taken from you one piece at a time, notification after notification. Listening to someone all the way through, without cutting in, without checking your phone: when was the last time?

Third: we've got no time. Five days holding your breath to get to the weekend, and on the weekend you dump all the worst of you. Caring about someone takes time, takes room, takes going slow. And we've decided speed is the value, to the point where stopping feels almost like a fault.

I'm not being poetic

Someone will say: nice, but that's poetry. No. These are numbers, and they're heavy.

Ours is a social brain, built over thousands of years to give and receive. Neuroscience says something worth keeping in mind: social pain, meaning being rejected, excluded, left alone, lights up the same areas of the brain as physical pain. Feeling lonely genuinely hurts. It's not a figure of speech, you can measure it.

There's the Harvard study on adult development, the longest one ever run on happiness: over eighty years following the same people. The conclusion leaves no way out. What makes you live long, healthy and content isn't money, isn't success, isn't fame. It's your relationships, and how good they are. Chronic loneliness, on the other hand, does damage comparable to smoking.

In Italy almost nine million people live alone. And plenty more have people around them and a loneliness inside that nobody sees. It's an epidemic no one talks about. Meanwhile someone has built an enormous business on top of it.

Look at addiction, same story. The opposite of addiction isn't sobriety, it's connection. When the bond is missing, when there's nobody who looks at you and gives you worth, your brain goes and gets its dopamine wherever it's cheap: substances, food, the screen. We mistake pleasure for happiness. Deep down, addiction is a desperate attempt to hook back onto something, or at least to stop feeling the emptiness.

The problem is it doesn't sell

So why, with all this evidence, does nobody put empathy at the centre? The answer is ugly but simple: there's no business behind it. You can't patent a friendship. You can't sell listening in pill form. You can't invoice a hug.

The health market sells you what can be sold: supplements, genetic tests, cold showers, pulsing red light, whichever longevity guru is up. The thing with the most scientific evidence of all, a real human relationship, stays out of the loop. It's free, it's rare, and when it's fake you feel it instantly: we have an antenna for exactly that. Which is why it's hard even to fake, for artificial intelligence too. And in a market, whatever is free, rare and impossible to copy becomes invisible. Even if it's the most precious thing we have.

And if you're the empathic one, you pay

There's one last piece, the bitterest. This society doesn't just fail to reward empathy: it actively punishes it. The most sensitive people, the many neurodivergent heads that work like antennas and feel other people's emotions as if they were their own, end up with labels like fragile, too sensitive, faulty. So they learn to harden, to defend themselves by going tough.

Meanwhile we reward speed, the ones who push themselves forward, multitasking (which, by the way, doesn't exist), stress resistance. All that resilience talk that most of the time means one thing only: change nothing and hold out inside a situation that's hurting you.

The trouble is empathy works one way only. You've got a single tool, and you use it on yourself and on other people. If I go numb to my own pain to protect myself, I go numb to yours too. I can't be kind to you if I'm not kind to me. And when we numb ourselves, because we're full of knocks, stress, stuff to carry, we switch other people off as well. That's where empathy gets lost, one piece at a time.

That's why compassion isn't do-goodery, isn't weakness, isn't a luxury for delicate souls. It's the oldest thing we know how to do, and we need it because on our own we don't manage. We're not built to be alone.

And more than that, we're not interchangeable. You don't lose one person and find another, the way you swap out a broken part. Everyone pulls something different out of you: there's the one who makes you laugh like nobody else, the one you can sit in silence with without it being awkward, the one who tells you the truth when everyone else talks around it. And when one of those people disappears, you haven't lost a contact in your phone. You've lost the part of you that only came out with them.

I'll leave you with a few questions I've been carrying around. When was the last time someone really listened to you, all the way through, without interrupting and without looking at their phone? And when was the last time you did it for someone else? When was the last evening you spent in company so good that you checked neither the clock nor your phone?

And then the uncomfortable one, worth asking anyway: who disappeared from your life without you even noticing? Who didn't you miss? Because that tells you something too, about you and about what that relationship really was.
