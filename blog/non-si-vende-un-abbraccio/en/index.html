You can't sell a hug

We all say the same thing: we have everything. Never before has humanity had so much technology, so much information, so many ways to reach anyone, anywhere, at any time. And yet there's an emptiness running through our days, and we pretend not to see it.

When I stop and ask myself what is really missing, only one answer comes to mind: real compassion and empathy. The authentic kind. Of fake empathy we have far too much: the little hearts, the «I'm here for you», the lines tossed off in three seconds between one notification and the next, now written by artificial intelligence too. That kind we have in abundance, and it's useless.

That's the whole paradox. We live inside a stream of relationships that are fast, shallow, intermittent, and we feel it. We've become very good at telling when someone is truly interested in us and when they're just waiting for their turn to speak. Connected on the surface, never really close.

A brain built for another world

There's a word I use a lot: unnatural. Our brain took shape in small groups, at slow rhythms, face to face. Now that same brain finds itself in an environment that changes at an absurd speed, a place that is essentially alien to it.

This gap, between how we're built and how we live, is where much of the suffering I see around me begins. Because suffering, almost always, is not a defect to repair inside us. It sits in the space between us and our environment. And what kind of environment is this?

It's a competitive environment. There's an implicit, constant race between all of us, fed by the social comparison that social media has multiplied beyond measure. Other people stop being fellow travellers and become yardsticks, occasions for envy. You can't feel compassion for someone your brain is using as a benchmark, in a race no one chose but everyone follows.

It's a distracted environment. Empathy needs attention, and attention is exactly what the digital economy takes from us a little at a time, notification after notification. Listening to someone all the way through, without interrupting, without checking your phone, has become a rare event.

And it's a claustrophobic environment. Five days holding our breath to reach the weekend, where we then dump all the worst of ourselves. Compassion needs time, space, slowness. And we've turned speed into a value, to the point that stopping feels almost like a fault.

It's not poetry, it's data

Here someone will say: nice, but it's poetry. No. It's science, and the numbers are heavy.

Ours is a social brain, built over thousands of years to give and receive. Neuroscience tells us something we should keep in mind: social pain, that is rejection, exclusion, loneliness, lights up the same brain areas as physical pain. Feeling alone genuinely hurts. It's a concrete, measurable pain.

The Harvard study on adult development, the longest ever run on human happiness, over eighty years of observation, reaches a conclusion that leaves no way out: what predicts a long, healthy, happy life isn't money, isn't success, isn't fame. It's relationships, and their quality. Chronic loneliness, on the other hand, does damage to health comparable to smoking.

In Italy almost nine million people live alone, and many more live with a loneliness that doesn't show. It's a silent epidemic. And around this loneliness some have built an enormous business.

Even addiction tells the same story. The opposite of addiction isn't sobriety: it's connection. When the bond is missing, when there's no one who looks at you and gives you value, the brain goes looking for cheap dopamine wherever it can find it: in substances, in food, in the screen. We mistake pleasure for happiness. Deep down, addiction is a desperate attempt to reconnect, or at least to numb the emptiness.

The problem is that it doesn't sell

So why, with all this evidence, does no one put empathy at the centre? The answer is brutal: because there's no business model behind it. You can't patent a friendship, you can't sell real listening in pills, you can't simulate a hug.

The health market sells you sellable things: supplements, genetic tests, cold showers, pulsing red light, the longevity guru of the moment. The variable with the most scientific evidence of all, a real human relationship, stays off the radar. It's free, it's rare, and when it isn't authentic we feel it instantly: we have antennas for exactly that. Which is why, at least for now, it's hard to simulate even for artificial intelligence. And in a market society, whatever is free, rare and non-replicable ends up becoming invisible. Even when it's the most precious thing we have.

The empathy we punish

There's one last step, the bitterest. This society doesn't just fail to reward empathy: it punishes it. The most sensitive people, the many neurodivergent minds that work like antennas and feel others' emotions as if they were their own, get labelled fragile, too sensitive, defective. So they learn to harden, to defend themselves by becoming tough.

Meanwhile we reward speed, assertiveness, multitasking (which, by the way, doesn't exist), stress resistance. That rhetoric of resilience that too often means one thing only: change nothing and endure a situation that is hurting you.

The trouble is that empathy works one way only. We have a single tool, and we use it both on ourselves and on others. If, to protect myself, I become numb to my own pain, I become numb to yours too. I can't be kind to you if I'm not kind to myself. And when we anaesthetise our own pain, from too much trauma, too much stress, too much load, we switch off other people's too. That's where empathy gets lost, a little at a time.

That's why compassion isn't do-goodery, isn't weakness, isn't a luxury for beautiful souls. It's the oldest and most powerful technology our species has ever built, because on our own we don't make it. We're not meant to be singularities.

I'll leave you with the question I've been carrying around for a while. When was the last time someone truly listened to you, all the way through, without interrupting and without looking at their phone? And when was the last time you did it for someone else?
